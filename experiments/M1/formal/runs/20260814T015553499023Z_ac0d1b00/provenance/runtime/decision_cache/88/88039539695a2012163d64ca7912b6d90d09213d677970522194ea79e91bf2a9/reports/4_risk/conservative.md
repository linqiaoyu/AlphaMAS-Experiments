It is just as likely that buyers are absent as it is that sellers are exhausted. The VWMA at $186.93 being slightly above spot is a marginal signal at best—it does not indicate accumulation. If we look at the price action, the stock has been oscillating between roughly $185.5 and $188 for over a week. That is a range, not a breakout setup. You are interpreting the absence of selling as a reason to buy, but the absence of buying is equally plausible. Without a volume expansion on an upward move, we have no evidence that the market is willing to push this stock higher.

Third, your fundamental argument is the most dangerous. You say we don’t need income statement data because operating cash flow of $39.9 billion is proof of a “cash machine.” That is true—Apple is a cash machine. But that does not mean the stock is a buy at this price. Cash flow strength does not tell us about revenue growth, margins, or earnings quality. We have no idea if that cash flow is sustainable, if margins are compressing, or if the company is facing competitive pressures. The fact that the income statement is unavailable is not a trivial gap—it is a critical missing piece. You are asking us to make a directional bet on a stock without knowing whether the underlying business is growing or stagnating. That is not aggressive; that is reckless. A conservative approach demands that we have a complete picture before committing additional capital.

It means we have no visibility into whether there are upcoming catalysts, regulatory risks, or macro shocks that could hit this stock. You say “no negative news means the path of least resistance is up.” That is a logical fallacy. No news means we are flying blind. In a blind environment, the prudent action is to reduce risk, not increase it. The fact that the stock is below its 50-day SMA and the medium-term trend is soft should give us pause, not confidence.

Meanwhile, the downside to $179.50 is a clear level—it is below the 200-day SMA and the Bollinger lower band. If that level breaks, there is no telling how far the stock could fall. The asymmetry you describe is only favorable if you assign a high probability to the upside, and the evidence does not support that.

Each of those levels could fail in a low-volume, no-catalyst environment. You are asking us to catch a falling knife based on a hope that the 200-day SMA will hold. That is not a strategy; it is a gamble.

Finally, you challenge us to provide evidence that AAPL will break down. I will not claim it will. What I will say is that the burden of proof is on those who want to take on more risk. The current evidence is balanced—price is above the 200-day SMA but below the 50-day SMA, momentum is neutral, volume is low, and fundamentals are incomplete. In a balanced situation, the correct action is to hold, not to add. The firm’s objective is to protect assets and ensure steady growth. Adding to a position in a range-bound stock with no confirmed catalyst and no volume expansion does not meet that objective. It introduces volatility and downside risk for a marginal expected gain.

I will not be swayed by the argument that we should “buy before the easy 3% gain is gone.” That is timing the market, and timing the market is a loser’s game. Until then, holding the current position with a defined stop loss is the only prudent action.
